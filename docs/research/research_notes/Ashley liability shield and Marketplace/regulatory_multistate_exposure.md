## 1.1 FTC Act §5, ROSCA, the negative-option ("click-to-cancel") rule in 2026, state automatic-renewal laws, and the FTC "free" guides [PARTIAL — sub-research stopped; only independently verified items are findings]

### Takeaway
There is no federal click-to-cancel rule in force on 2026-09-28. The 2024 Negative Option Rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit in July 2025 for want of the preliminary regulatory analysis that FTC Act §18 requires, and the FTC started over: a draft ANPRM went to OMB on 30 January 2026, the ANPRM was announced 11 March 2026 and published 13 March 2026, comments closed 13 April 2026, and the Commission has committed only to deciding later whether to proceed to a proposed rule, so nothing new can plausibly be in force before 2027. ROSCA and the 1973 prenotification rule remain and are being enforced, but both reach only negative-option (recurring or auto-converting) features. A free trial that does not convert and a one-time paid licence are outside ROSCA and outside every state automatic-renewal law seen here (California's §17602 requires a retention-capable acknowledgment only for automatic-renewal or continuous-service offers). The one "free"-claim rule verified is Michigan's MCPA 445.903(1)(r); the federal 16 CFR 251 guides, the FTC deception/unfairness statements and the 2024–2026 dark-pattern enforcement record were not retrieved and are in Gaps.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- With no recurring charge, no stored card and no automatic conversion, Ashley has no ROSCA, negative-option-rule or state-ARL obligation today. The design consequence is to keep it that way by construction: the trial collects no payment credentials at all, and the paid licence is a single charge for a stated term. If a subscription or an auto-converting trial is ever introduced, three regimes arrive together — ROSCA's pre-billing disclosure / express-consent / simple-cancellation triad, the state ARLs (California's affirmative consent, retention-capable acknowledgment and same-medium cancellation), and E-SIGN §7001(c) for the retention-capable acknowledgment (§6.1) — and that should be run as a separate compliance project, not a pricing toggle.
- The ANPRM's questions (cancellation timeframes, "save" offers, disclosure and consent) track the vacated rule's core; a future rule will very probably restore clear-and-conspicuous pre-consent disclosure, express informed consent and a cancellation path as easy as sign-up. Building the checkout to that pattern costs nothing for a one-time product and future-proofs a later subscription.
- Section 5's deception baseline (not verified here; Gaps) still governs the trial: whatever the listing says about length, features and what happens at expiry must be true and must not be contradicted by anything in the licence text.

## 2.1 Michigan Consumer Protection Act (MCL 445.901–.922)
### Takeaway
The MCPA applies to this vendor. The "specifically authorized" exemption (MCL 445.904(1)(a), as read by Smith v. Globe Life and Liss v. Lewiston-Richards) removes conduct that a regulator authorises; selling software licences online is not a licensed or regulated activity, so the exemption does not reach it. A Michigan student can sue for the greater of actual damages or $250 plus attorney fees, with a 6-year limitations period and no pre-suit notice. Class actions are limited to actual damages. Several listed practices bite directly on EULA drafting: (n) confusion about legal rights, (t) waivers not "clearly stated" and "specifically consented to", (y) failure to provide promised benefits, (u) failing to promptly restore payments after a cancellation the agreement permits, and (r) the "free" disclosure rule for the trial.

### Cited Findings
- Private action: "A person who suffers loss as a result of a violation of this act may bring an action" for declaratory judgment, injunction, or damages; individual recovery is "actual damages or $250.00, whichever is greater, together with reasonable attorney fees"; class actions are "on behalf of persons residing or injured in this state for the actual damages"; limitations: not "more than 6 years after the occurrence" or "more than 1 year after the last payment," whichever is later; no pre-suit notice appears in the section; last amended 2020 PA 296, eff. 2021-04-01 — [MCL 445.911, Michigan Legislature](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-445-911) 2026-09-28
- Listed practices, verbatim from the statute (last amended 2022 PA 152, eff. 2023-01-01):
  - (1)(n) "Causing a probability of confusion or of misunderstanding as to the legal rights, obligations, or remedies of a party to a transaction."
  - (1)(r) "Representing that a consumer will receive goods or services free or without charge, or using words of similar import in the representation, without clearly and conspicuously disclosing with equal prominence in immediate conjunction with the use of those words the conditions, terms, or prerequisites to the use or retention of the goods or services advertised."
  - (1)(s) "Failing to reveal a material fact, the omission of which tends to mislead or deceive the consumer, and which fact could not reasonably be known by the consumer."
  - (1)(t) "Entering into a consumer transaction in which the consumer waives or purports to waive a right, benefit, or immunity provided by law, unless the waiver is clearly stated and the consumer has specifically consented to it."
  - (1)(u) "Failing, in a consumer transaction that is rescinded, canceled, or otherwise terminated in accordance with the terms of an agreement, advertisement, representation, or provision of law, to promptly restore to the person or persons entitled to it a deposit, down payment, or other payment ..."
  - (1)(y) "Gross discrepancies between the oral representations of the seller and the written agreement covering the same transaction or failure of the other party to the transaction to provide the promised benefits."
  - (1)(bb) "Making a representation of fact or statement of fact material to the transaction such that a person reasonably believes the represented or suggested state of affairs to be other than it actually is."
  - (1)(cc) "Failing to reveal facts that are material to the transaction in light of representations of fact made in a positive manner."
  — [MCL 445.903, Michigan Legislature](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-445-903) 2026-09-28
- Note: the numbered items the task guessed at do not match the statute. "Unconscionable terms" is not a separate lettered item; the waiver rule is (t); "failure to provide the promised benefits" is the second half of (y), not (bb); (cc) is a material-omission rule, not a cancellation/refund-disclosure rule. — same source, 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- The exemption test (MCL 445.904(1)(a)) as read in Smith v. Globe Life Ins. Co., 460 Mich. 446 (1999) and Liss v. Lewiston-Richards, Inc., 478 Mich. 203 (2007) asks whether the general transaction is specifically authorised by law, not whether the specific misconduct is. An unlicensed software vendor has no licensing statute to point to, so the MCPA applies in full. (Case cites from general knowledge; opinions not fetched this session. Section text: https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-445-904 — URL not fetched this session.)
- Drafting consequences: (t) means a warranty disclaimer or liability cap in a click-wrap is safest when it is set apart, conspicuous, and accepted by an affirmative act; (n) means the EULA should not overstate what the vendor may do (e.g. "no refunds under any circumstances" when Michigan law gives a remedy); (r) means the free-trial page must state the trial's conditions (duration, what happens at expiry, whether a card is taken) in immediate conjunction with the word "free"; (u) means that if the EULA itself allows any cancellation, refunds owed under it must be paid promptly.
- Michigan has no general statute requiring a posted refund policy for online sales; the closest hooks are (r), (s), (u) and (cc) above, and the FTC Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule (16 CFR 435) for shipping delays, which is not relevant to instant download.

### Gaps
- MCL 445.904 text and the two exemption opinions were not fetched; verify the wording before citing. Whether a court would treat a paid VS Code extension licence as "primarily for personal, family, or household purposes" (MCL 445.902 "trade or commerce") is a fact question when the student uses it for coursework; no authority located either way.
- MCL 445.911 also lets a court award "reasonable attorney fees" but says nothing on fee-shifting to a prevailing defendant; not investigated.

## 2.2 California: CLRA, UCL, FAL, Civ. Code 1670.8 / 1671 / 1670.5 / 1668, B&P 17538
### Takeaway
California is the most demanding state for this EULA. The CLRA gives a 30-day notice-and-cure window that a vendor can use to defeat damages (not injunctive) claims; the class-action floor is $1,000 total, punitive damages and mandatory fees are available; its anti-waiver clause (1751) plus America Online v. Superior Court means a Michigan choice-of-law/forum clause will not strip a California consumer of CLRA rights. Civ. Code 1670.8 voids non-disparagement clauses and imposes penalties of $2,500/$5,000/$10,000; the CRFA does the same federally (see §2.3). B&P 17538(d) requires an internet vendor to disclose its legal name, complete street address and refund policy before taking payment; its "goods" definition is "tangible chattels", so coverage of a downloadable licence is unconfirmed, but posting the three items costs nothing and removes the argument.

### Cited Findings
- CLRA notice-and-cure: "Thirty days or more prior to the commencement of an action for damages pursuant to this title, the consumer shall" give written notice of the specific violations and demand correction; sent "by certified or registered mail, return receipt requested, to the place where the transaction occurred or to the person's principal place of business within California"; "No action for damages may be maintained under Section 1780 if an appropriate correction, repair, replacement, or other remedy is given, or agreed to be given within a reasonable time, to the consumer within 30 days after receipt of the notice"; class cure under (c) requires identifying similarly situated consumers, notifying them, giving the remedy and ceasing the practice; (d): "An action for injunctive relief brought under the specific provisions of Section 1770 may be commenced without compliance with subdivision (a)" and may later be amended to add damages after notice — [Cal. Civ. Code 1782, leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1782) 2026-09-28
- CLRA remedies: actual damages, but in a class action the total award may not "be less than one thousand dollars ($1,000)"; injunction; restitution; punitive damages; "any other relief that the court deems proper"; up to $5,000 additional for seniors/disabled; venue includes the county where the defendant "is doing business" or "where the transaction or any substantial portion thereof occurred"; "The court shall award court costs and attorney's fees to a prevailing plaintiff" — [Cal. Civ. Code 1780, leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1780) 2026-09-28
- Non-disparagement: a consumer contract may not "waive the consumer's right to make any statement regarding the seller or lessor or its employees or agents"; unlawful "to threaten or to seek to enforce a provision made unlawful under this section, or to otherwise penalize a consumer"; civil penalty up to $2,500 first violation, up to $5,000 each subsequent, up to $10,000 if "willful, intentional, or reckless"; enforceable by the consumer, the Attorney General, a district attorney or city attorney; "Any waiver of the provisions of this section is contrary to public policy, and is void and unenforceable." — [Cal. Civ. Code 1670.8, leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1670.8) 2026-09-28
- Internet-seller disclosure: (d) before accepting payment an internet vendor must disclose "the vendor's return and refund policy, the legal name under which the business is conducted" and "the complete street address from which the business is actually conducted"; permitted placements include "the first screen displayed when the vendor's electronic site is accessed", the screen where goods are first offered or the order is placed, "for nonbrowser-based technologies, in a manner that gives the user a reasonable opportunity to review", or by e-mail within five days on request. Definitions: goods = "tangible chattels, including certificates or coupons exchangeable for those goods"; services = "work, labor, and services, including services furnished in connection with the sale or repair of goods". Violation is a misdemeanor: up to six months county jail and/or a fine up to $1,000. The statute does not mention downloadable software. — [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code 17538, leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17538) 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- Cure strategy: on receipt of a CLRA letter, an offer within 30 days of a full refund (and, if the complaint is about a term, an undertaking to drop or rewrite it) removes the damages claim for that consumer; the injunctive claim survives, which is why the clause itself should be fixed rather than defended.
- Anti-waiver / choice-of-law: Civ. Code 1751 ("Any waiver by a consumer of the provisions of this title is contrary to public policy and shall be unenforceable and void") and America Online, Inc. v. Superior Court (Mendoza), 90 Cal. App. 4th 1 (2001) (Virginia forum/choice-of-law clause unenforceable because it would waive CLRA remedies) mean the Michigan clause will not bar CLRA claims by a California buyer. Cites from general knowledge; 1751 URL pattern https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1751 — not fetched this session.
- 1770(a)(14) ("Representing that a transaction confers or involves rights, remedies, or obligations that it does not have or involve, or that are prohibited by law") is the CLRA hook for an over-broad disclaimer or a "no refunds ever" line; 1770(a)(19) (unconscionable provision in a contract) is the other. Not fetched; verify numbering.
- Liquidated damages: Civ. Code 1671(d) makes a liquidated-damages clause in a consumer contract void "except that the parties ... may agree therein upon an amount which shall be presumed to be the amount of damage sustained by a breach thereof, when, from the nature of the case, it would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix the actual damage" — a licence-fee-multiple penalty for sharing a key will very likely fail this test. Civ. Code 1668 voids contracts exempting a party from "fraud, or willful injury ... or violation of law". Civ. Code 1670.5 lets a court refuse an unconscionable clause. All three from general knowledge; not fetched.
- UCL (B&P 17200) reaches "unlawful, unfair or fraudulent" practices, remedies are restitution and injunction only (no damages), 4-year limitations (17208); FAL (17500) covers untrue or misleading advertising, including "free" claims. A violation of any statute above becomes an "unlawful"-prong UCL claim. General knowledge; not fetched.
- B&P 17538(d): because "goods" means tangible chattels and "services" means labour, a pure downloadable licence may be outside the section; but the disclosure is trivial (legal name, street address, refund policy on the storefront or first screen) and a sole proprietor should note the street-address requirement is a real address, not a P.O. box — a home-based vendor may want a registered-agent or virtual-office address and should ask counsel whether that satisfies "actually conducted". Civ. Code 1723 (posted refund policy for retail sellers) is written for stores that accept returns of goods and was not fetched; treat as not applicable but unconfirmed.

## 2.3 New York GBL 349/350 and the federal Consumer Review Fairness Act
### Takeaway
GBL 349 gives a private action for consumer-oriented, materially misleading conduct causing injury: the greater of actual damages or $50, discretionary trebling up to $1,000 for willful/knowing violations, discretionary attorney's fees, no pre-suit consumer notice. The transaction must occur in New York (Goshen). Separately, the federal CRFA (15 U.S.C. 45b) voids any non-disparagement or review-restricting term in a form contract from the outset and makes offering such a contract a rule violation enforceable by the FTC and state AGs — so a non-disparagement clause should be dropped everywhere, not just for California.

### Cited Findings
- GBL 349(h): "any person who has been injured by reason of any deceptive act or deceptive practice made unlawful by this section may bring an action" to enjoin and "to recover his actual damages or fifty dollars, whichever is greater"; the court may "increase the award of damages to an amount not to exceed three times the actual damages up to one thousand dollars, if the court finds the defendant willfully or knowingly violated this section"; "The court may award reasonable attorney's fees to a prevailing plaintiff." 349(d): "it shall be a complete defense that the act or practice is, or if in interstate commerce would be, subject to and complies with the rules and regulations of, and the statutes administered by, the federal trade commission". The only notice provision (349(c)) is the Attorney General's ten-day notice before an AG suit; there is no consumer pre-suit notice. — [NY GBL 349, NY Senate](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/349) 2026-09-28
- CRFA: enacted 2016-12-14. A "form contract" is one with standardized terms "used by a person in the course of selling or leasing the person's goods or services" without a meaningful opportunity to negotiate; a "covered communication" is "a written, oral, or pictorial review, performance assessment of, or other similar analysis of" goods, services or conduct. Void from inception: provisions that prohibit or restrict such reviews, impose a penalty or fee for them, or transfer IP in them (a non-exclusive licence to use the content is allowed). "It shall be unlawful for a person to offer a form contract containing a provision described as void" in (b); a violation is treated as a violation of a rule under FTC Act §18. Exceptions preserve confidentiality duties, defamation claims, trade secrets, removal of unlawful/abusive/unrelated content, etc. — [15 U.S.C. 45b, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/45b) 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- GBL 350 (false advertising) tracks 349 with the same $50 floor / $500 treble cap in 350-e; it reaches a "free trial" claim that hides conditions. Not fetched; verify 350-e numbers.
- Territorial reach: Goshen v. Mutual Life Ins. Co., 98 N.Y.2d 314 (2002) requires that "the transaction in which the consumer is deceived must occur in New York"; a NY student buying from a NY device is a New York transaction, so a Michigan vendor is within reach. Cite from general knowledge; not fetched.
- NY's 2024–2026 online-contract statutes (automatic-renewal GBL 527-a, the "click to cancel" rules) attach to subscriptions; a one-time licence is outside them, but if the free trial converts automatically to a paid licence, treat it as a negative-option and re-check. Not researched.
- CRFA drafting rule: no clause that forbids reviews, penalises them (that includes a liquidated-damages figure tied to "disparagement"), or takes ownership of review content. A confidentiality clause covering trade secrets or licence keys is fine.

### Gaps

## 2.4 Illinois: ICFA (815 ILCS 505) and UDTPA (815 ILCS 510)
### Takeaway
ICFA gives a private action for "actual damage" with discretionary actual economic damages, punitive damages, injunctive relief and attorney's fees to the prevailing party (either side), 3-year limitations, no pre-suit notice. Reach is limited to transactions occurring "primarily and substantially" in Illinois (Avery), which an Illinois student buying from Illinois will satisfy. The UDTPA gives injunctive relief only.

### Cited Findings
- 815 ILCS 505/10a(a): "Any person who suffers actual damage as a result of a violation of this Act committed by any other person may bring an action against such person. The court, in its discretion may award actual economic damages or any other relief which the court deems proper" (punitive damages limited only for vehicle dealers / retail-instalment holders); 10a(c): "the Court may grant injunctive relief where appropriate and may award, in addition to the relief provided in this Section, reasonable attorney's fees and costs to the prevailing party" — text as reproduced in search results pointing to [ILGA 815 ILCS 505/10a](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081505050K10a.htm) and [Justia 815 ILCS 505](https://law.justia.com/codes/illinois/chapter-815/act-815-ilcs-505/) 2026-09-28 (direct ILGA fetch failed on a TLS certificate error; wording taken from the search snippet)

### Inferences
- Because fees go to "the prevailing party", a consumer who loses can be ordered to pay; courts read this narrowly against consumers absent bad faith, but it is a real deterrent to weak claims.
- Avery v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 216 Ill. 2d 100 (2005): ICFA does not apply to fraudulent transactions that take place outside Illinois; test is whether the circumstances relating to the transaction occur primarily and substantially in Illinois. General knowledge; not fetched.
- 815 ILCS 505/10a(e): 3-year limitations period. Not verified this session.
- UDTPA (815 ILCS 510/3): injunctive relief and, in the court's discretion, fees where the defendant "wilfully engaged" in the practice; no damages. General knowledge; not fetched.

## 2.5 New Jersey: CFA (N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.) and TCCWNA (56:12-14 to -18)
### Takeaway
The CFA gives treble damages and mandatory attorney's fees to a consumer who proves an "ascertainable loss". TCCWNA is the drafting trap: a consumer contract may not contain a provision that violates a clearly established legal right, and 56:12-16 forbids saying a provision "is or may be void, unenforceable or inapplicable in some jurisdictions" without specifying which are or are not void in New Jersey — so "to the extent permitted by law", "void where prohibited", and generic severability lines can be pleaded as violations. Remedy: civil penalty not less than $100 or actual damages, plus fees. Since Spade v. Select Comfort (2018) only an "aggrieved consumer" who suffered some adverse consequence can recover, which killed most no-injury class actions but not the exposure to an individual who was actually affected. Safe drafting: a New Jersey-specific paragraph that names which limitations do not apply to NJ residents.

### Cited Findings
- 56:12-16: a consumer contract, notice or sign may not "state that any of [its] provisions is or may be void, unenforceable or inapplicable in some jurisdictions without specifying which provisions are or are not void, unenforceable or inapplicable within the State of New Jersey." Plaintiffs have attacked "unless prohibited by law," "to the extent allowed by law," and "except as required by law" as triggering the duty to specify — [Faegre Drinker, "The Landmines Lurking in Your Website" (2016)](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2016/4/the-landmines-lurking-in-your-website) 2026-09-28
- 56:12-17: a violator "shall be liable to the aggrieved consumer for a civil penalty of not less than $100.00 or for actual damages, or both ... together with reasonable attorneys' fees and court costs" — as quoted in [Lowenstein Sandler client alert on Spade](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/new-jersey-supreme-court-significantly-limits-tccwna-claims-class-action-litigation) and [National Law Review, "No Harm No Foul"](https://natlawreview.com/article/no-harm-no-foul-nj-supreme-court-rejects-no-injury-gotcha-suits-under-tccwna) 2026-09-28 (Justia statute page returned 403; statute text via secondary sources)
- Spade v. Select Comfort Corp., A-57-16 (N.J. 2018): elements are (1) defendant is a seller/lessor/creditor/lender/bailee, (2) it offered or entered a written consumer contract or displayed a notice/sign, (3) the writing contains a provision that "violates any clearly established legal right of a consumer or responsibility of a seller" under state or federal law, and (4) plaintiff is an "aggrieved consumer"; holding: a consumer who receives a contract with prohibited language "but who suffers no monetary or other harm as a result of that noncompliance, is not an 'aggrieved consumer'" — [Spade v. Select Comfort, Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/supreme-court/2018/a-57-16.html) and [NJ Courts opinion page](https://www.njcourts.gov/court-opinion/david-spade-v-select-comfort-corp-christopher-wenger-v-bobs-discount-furniture-llc) 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- CFA (56:8-19): "threefold the damages sustained" plus "reasonable attorneys' fees, filing fees and reasonable costs of suit" — mandatory once an ascertainable loss caused by an unlawful practice is shown; no pre-suit notice. General knowledge; section not fetched.
- TCCWNA "clearly established legal right" candidates in this EULA: a blanket "no refunds" line if NJ law gives a refund right in a specific situation; a non-disparagement clause (void under CRFA); a liability cap that purports to exclude liability for the vendor's own fraud or gross negligence; a class waiver outside an arbitration clause. Spade means a plaintiff needs an actual adverse consequence (e.g. was refused a refund), so the exposure is per-affected-consumer, not per-copy-of-the-EULA.
- Safe mechanism (used widely after the 2016 wave): remove "void where prohibited" and unqualified "to the extent permitted by law" phrasing; add a section titled "New Jersey residents" that states in plain words which of the warranty disclaimer, limitation of liability, and remedy limits do not apply to New Jersey consumers (e.g. "Nothing in Sections X–Y limits liability for the vendor's own fraud, wilful misconduct or gross negligence, or your rights under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act"). Counsel must settle the exact list; the point is that the contract itself answers the 56:12-16 question.

### Gaps
- 56:8-19 and 56:12-15 texts not fetched. No 2025–2026 NJ appellate decision on TCCWNA applied to software EULAs located; not searched further.

## 2.6 Massachusetts G.L. c. 93A §9 and 940 CMR 3.16 / 6.00
### Takeaway
A Massachusetts consumer must send a written demand 30 days before suing unless the vendor "does not maintain a place of business or does not keep assets within the commonwealth" — which describes this Michigan vendor, so the demand requirement will usually not apply against it, and with it goes the vendor's tender-cap protection. Where a demand is sent, a reasonable written tender within 30 days caps recovery at the tender and cuts off later fees. Damages are actual or $25, doubled or trebled for willful/knowing violations or a bad-faith refusal of a demand; fees are mandatory for a winning consumer.

### Cited Findings
- §9(3): "At least thirty days prior to the filing of any such action, a written demand for relief ... shall be mailed or delivered to any prospective respondent"; the demand requirement "shall not apply if ... the prospective respondent does not maintain a place of business or does not keep assets within the commonwealth"; a respondent who "within thirty days ... makes a written tender of settlement which is rejected by the claimant may ... limit any recovery to the relief tendered if the court finds that the relief tendered was reasonable"; recovery is "actual damages or twenty-five dollars, whichever is greater; or up to three but not less than two times such amount if the court finds ... a willful or knowing violation ... or that the refusal to grant relief upon demand was made in bad faith". §9(4): "The petitioner shall ... be awarded reasonable attorney's fees and costs", but fees "incurred after the rejection of a reasonable written offer of settlement" are denied — [G.L. c. 93A §9, malegislature.gov](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93A/Section9) 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- Even without a mandatory demand, a Massachusetts plaintiff's lawyer will usually send one because a bad-faith refusal supports multiple damages; the vendor should answer any 93A letter within 30 days with a reasonable written tender (typically a full refund plus a small sum), which is the only lever that caps exposure.
- 940 CMR 3.16 (general "unfair or deceptive" catch-all: e.g. failing to disclose a fact that might influence the purchase decision) and 940 CMR 6.00 (retail advertising; 6.05 on "free" offers and price comparisons) apply to advertising directed at Massachusetts consumers regardless of medium; a "free trial" whose conditions are not disclosed at the offer is the obvious exposure. Regulations from general knowledge; not fetched this session.
- 93A §9 has no explicit anti-waiver text, but Massachusetts courts refuse to enforce choice-of-law clauses that would deny 93A protection where Massachusetts has the materially greater interest (Restatement §187 analysis). Not sourced this session; counsel decision.

## 2.7 Texas DTPA (Bus. & Com. Code 17.41–.63)
### Takeaway
A Texas consumer must give 60 days' written notice stating the specific complaint and the amount of economic damages, mental-anguish damages, and expenses including attorney's fees (17.505). The vendor may tender a settlement within 60 days (17.5052); if the consumer rejects it and the tender was the same as, substantially the same as, or more than what the fact-finder awards, damages are capped at the lesser of the tender or the finding, and fees are capped at fees incurred before the offer. A pre-dispute waiver in an EULA is ineffective: 17.42 requires a signed writing by a consumer who is not in a significantly disparate bargaining position and is represented by counsel, in a prescribed conspicuous form. Multipliers: up to three times economic damages for knowing/intentional conduct (17.50(b)(1)) — numbers not verified from the statute this session.

### Cited Findings
- 17.5052(a): "A person who receives notice under Section 17.505 may tender an offer of settlement at any time during the period beginning on the date the notice is received and ending on the 60th day after that date." 17.5052(g): "If the court finds that the amount tendered in the settlement offer for damages under Subsection (d)(1) is the same as, substantially the same as, or more than the damages found by the trier of fact, the consumer may not recover as damages any amount in excess of the lesser of: (1) the amount of damages tendered in the settlement offer; or (2) the amount of damages found by the trier of fact." 17.5052(h): if the fees tendered are "the same as, substantially the same as, or more than the amount of reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees incurred by the consumer as of the date of the offer, the consumer may not recover attorneys' fees greater than the amount of fees tendered in the settlement offer." — [Tex. Bus. & Com. Code 17.5052, texas.public.law mirror](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._and_com._code_section_17.5052) 2026-09-28
- Summary-level confirmation (official page, numbers not quoted verbatim by the fetch): 17.42 waiver must be in writing, signed, by a consumer not in a significantly disparate bargaining position who is represented by legal counsel, in conspicuous form; 17.50(b) allows economic damages, mental anguish and treble damages for knowing/intentional conduct; 17.505 requires 60-day written notice with specific complaint and amount and allows abatement; 17.49 exempts certain written contracts above $100,000 / $500,000 and professional services — [Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 17, statutes.capitol.texas.gov](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/htm/BC.17.htm) 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- The 17.49 exemptions (professional services; large written contracts) are irrelevant to a sub-$1,000 software licence.
- Reach: the DTPA applies to a "consumer" who "seeks or acquires by purchase or lease, any goods or services" (17.45(4)); "goods" includes "tangible chattels or real property" and "services" means "work, labor, or service purchased or leased for use" — whether a software licence is a DTPA "good" has been litigated with mixed results; not researched here. The DTPA has no express territorial limit and Texas courts apply it to out-of-state sellers who transact with Texas consumers, subject to personal jurisdiction (§2.8). General knowledge.
- Practical: any Texas letter should be answered within 60 days with a written tender itemising damages and fees separately (17.5052(d)); the tender is the only cap.

## 2.8 Choice-of-law and personal jurisdiction
### Takeaway
Michigan follows Restatement (Second) Conflict of Laws §187 (Chrysler v. Skyline, 1995): a chosen law governs unless the chosen state has no substantial relationship to the parties or applying it would contradict a fundamental policy of a state with a materially greater interest whose law would otherwise apply. Michigan has a substantial relationship (vendor's home), so the clause will usually govern ordinary contract questions. It will not displace another state's consumer-protection statute where that statute carries an anti-waiver rule (California CLRA 1751 / AOL v. Superior Court; Texas 17.42; New Jersey CFA/TCCWNA as applied by NJ courts) or where the forum treats its UDAP statute as fundamental policy. Expect the clause to be honoured for warranty, licence scope and damages-cap questions and disregarded for the consumer's home-state UDAP claim. Personal jurisdiction: a vendor that knowingly sells and delivers to a student in a state will very likely face specific jurisdiction there (Ford; Briskin v. Shopify en banc 2025).

### Cited Findings
- Chrysler Corp. v. Skyline Industrial Services, Inc., 448 Mich. 113, 528 N.W.2d 698 (1995): the Michigan Supreme Court agreed the Court of Appeals "was correct in considering the Restatement approach" (§§187-188) to a contractual choice-of-law clause, then applied Michigan law on the facts — [Chrysler v. Skyline, Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/michigan/supreme-court/1995/97396-6.html) and [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2028111/chrysler-corp-v-skyline-industrial-services-inc/) 2026-09-28
- Briskin v. Shopify, Inc. (9th Cir. en banc, 2025-04-21): a California resident's purchase through a Shopify-hosted storefront; the en banc court held Shopify "expressly aimed" conduct at California "through its extraction, maintenance, and commercial distribution of the California consumers' personal data in violation of California laws", abandoning the prior "differential targeting" requirement for online defendants — [Proskauer alert](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/ninth-circuit-reshapes-personal-jurisdiction-standards-for-e-commerce-platforms-in-briskin-v-shopify), [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/ninth-circuit-substantially-expands-personal-jurisdiction-over-e-commerce-businesses-in-briskin-v-shopify/), [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2025/04/ninth-circuit-takes-a-wrecking-ball-to-internet-personal-jurisdiction-law-briskin-v-shopify.htm) 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- Under Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 592 U.S. 351 (2021), a claim need only "arise out of or relate to" forum contacts; a vendor that accepts a student's order, knows the billing state, and delivers a licence there has purposefully availed itself of that state for that sale. The old Zippo sliding scale (interactive/commercial site) points the same way. After Briskin, the Ninth Circuit no longer requires that the site target one state differently from others. General knowledge for Ford/Zippo; not fetched.
- A Michigan forum-selection clause faces the same fundamental-policy screen; California courts (AOL v. Superior Court) and others have declined to enforce a foreign forum clause where the forum would not apply the consumer's home UDAP statute. Counsel should decide whether to keep a forum clause at all or make it non-exclusive.
- The Michigan choice-of-law clause is still worth having: it fixes the law for contract formation, licence scope, disclaimers and caps in the many states without anti-waiver rules, and it gives the vendor one body of law to draft against.

### Gaps
- Ford, Zippo, AOL v. Superior Court and any Washington or New Jersey choice-of-law consumer decisions were not fetched; Restatement §187 text not fetched. No 2026 Supreme Court or circuit decision on Briskin located (a cert. petition was reported; status unknown — verify).

## 2.9 Arbitration, class waiver, mass-arbitration cost, click-wrap assent
### Takeaway
Under the FAA as read in AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion (2011) a class waiver inside an arbitration clause is enforceable notwithstanding state unconscionability rules aimed at such waivers; California's McGill v. Citibank (2017) still voids a waiver of public injunctive relief, so a clause must carve that out. The vendor pays almost all of the cost of consumer arbitration under AAA rules: business filing fee $375, case management fee $1,400 (single arbitrator), hearing fee $500, plus all arbitrator compensation, against a consumer filing fee capped at $225. For a one-time licence priced in the tens of dollars, a single arbitration costs the vendor more than any plausible refund, and a coordinated batch of filings can be existential — this is a counsel decision, and the plain alternative (no arbitration clause, small-claims-friendly refund policy) is defensible for a tiny vendor. Whatever terms are used, they bind only if the interface gives reasonably conspicuous notice and the user takes an unambiguous act of assent (Nguyen/Meyer; Chabolla v. ClassPass 2025 rejecting a sign-in wrap; Edmundson v. Klarna 2023 enforcing a clear click-wrap).

### Cited Findings
- AAA Consumer fee schedule (2025): consumer filing fee "capped at $225", payable by the individual "unless the parties' agreement provides that the individual pay less"; business filing fee "$375 ... once the individual claimant meets the filing requirements"; case management fee "$1,400 for 1 arbitrator or $1,775 for 3 arbitrators ... assessed to the business ... at the time the arbitrator is presented"; hearing fee "$500 ... payable by the business", refunded if settled before hearing; "The company pays the remaining administrative fees and all arbitrator compensation in consumer cases." — [AAA Consumer Rules, Forms & Fee Schedule](https://www.adr.org/rules-forms-and-fees/consumer/) and [AAA fee schedule PDF](https://www.adr.org/media/3uofn4lu/consumer_rules_and_mediation_procedures_feeschedule.pdf) (numbers via search summary; the PDF's text could not be parsed by the fetch tool) 2026-09-28
- AAA revised its Consumer Arbitration Rules in 2025 and has separate Mass Arbitration Supplementary Rules and fees — [Troutman Pepper Locke, Consumer Financial Services Law Monitor (May 2025)](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2025/05/aaa-unveils-significant-revisions-to-consumer-arbitration-rules/) and [AAA Mass Arbitration Rules & Fees](https://www.adr.org/rules-forms-and-fees/mass-arbitration/) 2026-09-28
- Chabolla v. ClassPass Inc. (9th Cir. 2025-02-27, 2-1): users were not bound by a "sign-in wrap" that linked to terms but did not require reading them before purchase; assent requires "(1) the website provides reasonably conspicuous notice of the terms to which users will be bound; and (2) users take some action, such as clicking a button or checking a box, that unambiguously manifests their assent to those terms" — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/its-wrap-latest-ninth-circuit-sign-wrap-agreements), [Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/thats-a-wrap-ninth-circuit-signals-tougher-standards-for-enforceability-of-online-agreements), [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2025/03/the-ninth-circuit-has-a-lot-to-say-about-online-contract-formation-much-of-it-confusing-chabolla-v-classpass.htm) 2026-09-28
- Edmundson v. Klarna, Inc., 85 F.4th 695 (2d Cir. 2023-11-03) (not 2025): arbitration clause enforced where the interface placed "I agree to the payment terms" next to a "Confirm and continue" button, with "payment terms" underlined, bolded and hyperlinked — [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/thats-a-wrap-second-circuit-upholds-click-wrap-mandatory-arbitration-provision), [Kilpatrick](https://ktslaw.com/en/Blog/classaction/2023/11/Second-Circuit-reverses-district-court) 2026-09-28

### Inferences
- Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), and McGill v. Citibank, N.A., 2 Cal. 5th 945 (2017), are cited from general knowledge; not fetched. If an arbitration clause is used it should: exempt small-claims court; not waive public injunctive relief; state that the business pays AAA fees beyond the consumer's cap; include a batching/bellwether protocol for mass filings; and be presented as a separate, checked-box acceptance to survive Chabolla-type scrutiny in the Ninth Circuit.
- Arbitrator compensation is on top of the administrative fees above; AAA consumer arbitrators are commonly compensated per hearing day or by "desk arbitration" flat rates in the low four figures — exact 2025–2026 amounts were not readable from the PDF and are listed under Gaps.
- No Michigan appellate decision specific to click-wrap EULAs was located this session; Michigan federal courts have applied the same Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble, 763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014) / Meyer v. Uber Technologies, 868 F.3d 66 (2d Cir. 2017) framework (conspicuous notice + unambiguous assent). General knowledge; treat as unverified.
- For a sole proprietor, the realistic dispute is a chargeback or a small-claims filing, not a class action; a clear, honoured refund policy prevents most of both. Whether to include arbitration at all is a counsel decision that turns on expected volume and on whether the vendor can fund even one AAA proceeding.

- AAA arbitrator-compensation figures and mass-arbitration per-case fee tiers for 2025–2026 not retrieved (PDF unparsed). JAMS consumer minimum standards and fee schedule not researched. Concepcion, McGill, Nguyen, Meyer not fetched. ClassPass's rehearing petition status after February 2025 unknown.

## 4.1 COPPA — does it reach a developer tool marketed to university students, and what does the 18+ gate do?
### Takeaway
COPPA reaches only (a) operators of sites/services "directed to children" under 13 and (b) general-audience operators with *actual knowledge* they are collecting personal information from a child under 13. A VS Code extension marketed to university students is a general-audience service under the Rule's own multi-factor test (subject matter, visual content, language, marketing, audience composition), and the 2025 amendments did not change the actual-knowledge standard. The vendor's obligations are therefore: do not market to or design for children; do not build features that create actual knowledge you then ignore; and if you learn a user is under 13, stop collecting and delete (or obtain verifiable parental consent). The 18+ representation is a contract-capacity device, not a COPPA "age screen" — for a general-audience service no age screen is required, but if one is used it should be neutral (no default to 13+, no hint that under-13 is blocked) and its answer must be honoured.

### Cited Findings
- Scope: the Rule covers "operators of commercial websites and online services (including mobile apps and IoT devices) directed to children under 13 that collect, use, or disclose personal information from children" and "operators of general audience websites or online services with actual knowledge that they are collecting, using, or disclosing personal information from children under 13." — [FTC, Complying with COPPA: Frequently Asked Questions](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions) (fetched 2026-09-28; page notes "The COPPA Rule was amended on April 22, 2025").
- Neutral age screen (FTC FAQ, written for mixed-audience sites but the only official description of an acceptable screen): "Ask age information in a neutral manner, making sure the data entry point allows users to enter their age accurately and does not default to an age 13 or over" (e.g., free entry of month and year of birth); avoid "encouraging children to falsify age information"; use "a cookie ... to prevent children from back-buttoning to enter a different age." — same FTC FAQ (fetched 2026-09-28).
- Actual knowledge consequence: where an operator gains actual knowledge that a user is a child, "the operator must either meet COPPA's notice and parental consent requirements or delete the child's information." — same FTC FAQ (fetched 2026-09-28).
- 2025 amendments: published April 22, 2025; take effect June 23, 2025; "Operators subject to the COPPA Rule will have until April 22, 2026 to comply." Changes include separate verifiable parental consent for third-party disclosures, a written information-security program, a written data-retention policy (no indefinite retention; delete when no longer needed for the documented purpose), and biometric identifiers added to "personal information." — [Mayer Brown, FTC Announces Significant Amendments to COPPA](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/04/ftc-announces-significant-amendments-to-coppa) (April 2025); [White & Case, Unpacking the FTC's COPPA amendments](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/unpacking-ftcs-coppa-amendments-what-you-need-know) (2025); [Latham & Watkins client alert, May 14, 2025](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule).
- 2025 amendments, definitional detail: the Rule now codifies a "mixed audience website or online service" (directed to children but not as primary audience) and requires that any age determination "be done in a neutral manner that does not default to a set age or encourage visitors to falsify age information"; new "directed to children" factors were added — "marketing or promotional materials or plans, representations to consumers or third parties, reviews by users or third parties and the ages of users on similar websites or services"; the article records no change to the actual-knowledge standard for general-audience services; the retention policy must be "published in the operator's privacy notice." — [Hunton, FTC Publishes Final COPPA Rule Amendments](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments) (April 25, 2025; fetched 2026-09-28). Note: Hunton states the effective date as June 21, 2025 (60 days from publication); Mayer Brown, White & Case and Latham state June 23, 2025. The compliance date (April 22, 2026) is consistent across all four.
- Statute and rule (not fetched, standard citations): 15 U.S.C. §§ 6501–6506, [law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/chapter-91); 16 C.F.R. Part 312, [ecfr.gov](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312).

### Inferences
- Ashley is not "directed to children": its subject matter (software development), language, marketing (university students), pricing and channel (VS Code Marketplace) all point to an adult/late-teen developer audience. None of the added 2025 factors (marketing materials, representations, reviews, comparable-site demographics) cuts the other way unless the vendor markets to high-school coding programs. Counsel should confirm the marketing plan stays clear of K-12 channels; that is the one lever the vendor controls that could change the answer.
- The 18+ checkbox does not by itself create COPPA "actual knowledge" of anyone under 13; nor does it insulate the vendor if other signals do (e.g., a support ticket saying "I'm 12"). The safer design is to collect as little age data as possible: a single "I am 18 or older" affirmation, no date-of-birth field. If a DOB field is ever added, apply the FTC's neutral-screen rules and block-and-delete on an under-13 answer.
- Deletion duty applies only to data actually collected from the child; a license record with an email address is "personal information." Build a "delete this account" path that support can trigger.

## 4.2 FERPA and PPRA — does a personally installed, student-paid tool touch either?
### Takeaway
FERPA binds educational agencies and institutions that receive U.S. Department of Education funds; it reaches a vendor only derivatively, when the school discloses PII from "education records" to the vendor as a "school official" under a contract and retains "direct control." A tool the student installs and pays for personally, with no school contract, holds no education records (records "maintained by an educational agency or institution or by a party acting for the agency or institution") and the vendor is not a school official. ED's own guidance says its online-services guidance "does not address online services or social media that students may use in their personal capacity outside of school." PPRA is a K-12 survey/marketing statute aimed at ED-funded agencies; it does not apply.

### Cited Findings
- Education records are "records that are: (1) directly related to a student; and (2) maintained by an educational agency or institution or by a party acting for the agency or institution" (20 U.S.C. § 1232g(a)(4)(A); 34 CFR § 99.3); "Provider services are typically procured through a contract or formal written agreement"; the school-official exception requires that the provider (1) performs an institutional function the school would otherwise use employees for, (2) meets the school's annual-notice criteria, (3) "is under the direct control of the agency or institution with respect to the use and maintenance of education records," and (4) uses records only for authorized purposes (34 CFR § 99.31(a)(1)(i)); "When PII from education records is disclosed to the provider, FERPA still governs its use, and the school or district is responsible for its protection." FERPA rights transfer to the student at 18 or on entering a postsecondary institution ("eligible student"). — [ED/PTAC, Responsibilities of Third-Party Service Providers under FERPA, PTAC-FAQ-5](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/Vendor%20FAQ.pdf) (August 2015; fetched 2026-09-28).
- The companion school-facing guidance covers services "provided by a third-party to a school or district that students and/or their parents access via the Internet" and states it "does not address online services or social media that students may use in their personal capacity outside of school." It also states: "The PPRA applies to the programs and activities of a State educational agency (SEA), local educational agency (LEA), or other recipient of funds under any program funded by the U.S. Department of Education." — [ED/PTAC, Protecting Student Privacy While Using Online Educational Services: Requirements and Best Practices](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/resources/protecting-student-privacy-while-using-online-educational-services-requirements-and-best) (February 2014; fetched 2026-09-28).
- ED FAQ for instructors on using an online tool in a course (school-selected tools; PII flows only where the school provides it): [studentprivacy.ed.gov FAQ](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/i-want-use-online-tool-or-application-part-my-course-however-i-am-worried-it-violation-ferpa) (surfaced by search 2026-09-28; not fetched).
- Statute and rule (not fetched, standard citations): FERPA 20 U.S.C. § 1232g, 34 CFR Part 99; PPRA 20 U.S.C. § 1232h, 34 CFR Part 98 (PPRA's survey, "marketing" and physical-exam provisions address elementary and secondary students).

### Inferences
- With no school contract and no PII flowing from the institution, FERPA imposes nothing on the vendor. What the student types into the tool (their own code, notes, course material they hold) is the student's own information, not an education record maintained by the school. The vendor's privacy obligations toward that content come from consumer-privacy law and its own privacy statement, not FERPA.
- The picture changes if Ashley ever (a) integrates with the school's LMS, SSO or grid accounts using institutional credentials, or (b) is licensed or "recommended" by a department in a way that routes institutional PII to the vendor. At that point the school will want a school-official/data-protection addendum, and FERPA's use/redisclosure limits arrive by contract. Keep the personal-install channel contract-free and say so in the privacy statement ("Ashley is not provided by or on behalf of your school").
- PPRA: irrelevant — it regulates ED-funded agencies' surveys and marketing-data collection from elementary/secondary students, not a postsecondary vendor.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2025 PTAC document specifically titled for "apps students choose themselves" was located; the 2014/2015 documents (still the ones ED hosts) carry the operative statement. If the caller has a citation to a newer ED guidance on student-selected apps, it should be substituted.
- The PPRA scope statement was verified only through the PTAC 2014 document; the statute text (20 U.S.C. § 1232h) was not fetched.

## 4.4 Minors' contracts, the infancy doctrine and the 18+ representation
### Takeaway
A person under the age of majority may generally disaffirm a contract during minority and within a reasonable time after reaching majority; the counterparty cannot. The age of majority is 18 everywhere except Alabama and Nebraska (19) and Mississippi (21); Michigan's is 18 (MCL 722.52). An online 18+ checkbox does not reliably estop a minor: estoppel for age misrepresentation is state-specific and, in Michigan, the statutory bar (MCL 600.1403) is narrow — it is written for suits to recover goods/chattels and requires a *separate written instrument containing only the statement of age, date of signing and signature* (or an admission in open court). The online cases split: courts have refused to let a minor keep the benefit and repudiate the terms (iParadigms, district-court reasoning), have let minors disaffirm and demand refunds even after consuming the benefit (I.B. v. Facebook, under California Family Code § 6710), and have let a minor disaffirm an EULA to escape arbitration (Doe v. Epic Games). For a low-priced, per-person license the economic risk is bounded: disaffirmance unwinds the license and the vendor refunds the price. The mechanism is: 18+ affirmation at sign-up, e-mail confirmation loop, a written refund-on-disaffirmance policy, and no attempt to enforce arbitration/limitation clauses against a user who turns out to be a minor.

### Inferences
- MCL 600.1403 is unlikely to help the vendor: (i) it is framed around actions "for the recovery of goods, wares, merchandise or chattels" (a software license/refund dispute may not fit), and (ii) an in-app checkbox embedded in a dialog is not "a separate instrument containing only the statement of age, date of signing and the signature." A stand-alone, dated, signed age attestation could be designed to fit the statute, but it would be friction out of proportion to a low-priced license. Counsel should decide whether the statute is worth designing for.
- The realistic exposure is a refund and loss of any arbitration/class-waiver protection against a minor who disaffirms. Because disaffirmance returns the license (the vendor can revoke the key), the downside is the purchase price plus the cost of handling the request. Build the refund into policy rather than litigating it.
- The 18+ affirmation is still worth collecting: it defeats a later claim that the vendor *knew* it was dealing with a minor (relevant under COPPA and under the fraud-based estoppel doctrine in states that recognise it), and it supports the "necessaries"/"benefit retained" arguments where courts entertain them.
- Recommended mechanism (design, not legal advice): (1) "I am 18 or older and I accept the License" as an affirmative, un-prechecked control; (2) e-mail verification of the account used to buy; (3) a published policy: a purchaser who was under 18 at purchase may cancel and receive a full refund on request, license revoked; (4) do not invoke arbitration or limitation clauses against a self-identified minor; (5) log the affirmation with the acceptance record (see §6.1).

## 5.1 Accessibility — ADA Title III, DOJ posture, Section 504/508, Unruh, New York, WCAG and VS Code UI
### Takeaway
There is no federal regulation setting a technical web/app standard for private businesses under ADA Title III; DOJ's 2022 guidance (still live on ada.gov as of 2026-09-28) says Title III applies to the web offerings of public accommodations and points to WCAG, and the eleven Title III guidance documents DOJ withdrew in March 2025 did not include it. Whether a software product with no physical storefront is a "place of public accommodation" is a circuit split: the First and Seventh Circuits say no nexus is needed; the Third, Sixth (Michigan; Parker v. Metropolitan Life, 1997: "A public accommodation is a physical place"), Ninth and Eleventh (Gil v. Winn-Dixie vacated) require a nexus; Second Circuit district courts are split. California's Unruh Act follows the nexus rule in state court (Martinez v. Cot'n Wash, 2022) and carries $4,000 statutory damages per violation; New York is the highest-volume venue and E.D.N.Y. has held a website itself is a public accommodation (Andrews v. Blick Art, 2017). The DOJ Title II rule (WCAG 2.1 AA; compliance now April 26, 2027 / April 26, 2028 after the April 20, 2026 IFR) and the HHS Section 504 rule (WCAG 2.1 AA; now May 11, 2027 / May 10, 2028 after the May 2026 IFR) do not bind a private vendor selling to individuals, but they bind public universities and HHS-funded schools, which will demand WCAG conformance before any official adoption. The exposure-reducing floor is to meet WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA in the first-run dialog and license flow, and to prefer VS Code's native UI (notifications, Quick Picks, walkthroughs) over a webview, exactly as VS Code's own guidelines say.

## 6.1 E-SIGN, UETA (Michigan), and retention of the accepted record
### Takeaway
E-SIGN § 7001(a) and Michigan UETA make the click-wrap license valid as an electronic record and its acceptance an electronic signature; nothing turns on paper. E-SIGN's consumer-consent regime, § 7001(c), is triggered only when a statute, regulation or rule of law *requires* information to be provided to a consumer *in writing*; a license acceptance that no law requires to be in writing does not trigger it. It would be triggered if the vendor ever offers an auto-renewing subscription (state ARLs, e.g., Cal. B&P § 17602(a)(3), require an acknowledgment "in a manner that is capable of being retained by the consumer") or has to make a legally-mandated written disclosure. Michigan UETA applies only between parties who have agreed to transact electronically (determined from context and conduct, MCL 450.835), and — critically for a dialog-only license — MCL 450.838(3): "If a sender inhibits the ability of a recipient to store or print an electronic record, the electronic record is not enforceable against the recipient." So the license text must be viewable, copyable, printable and reachable by URL, not locked inside a modal. No statute requires the vendor to retain proof of acceptance, but the vendor bears the burden of proving conspicuous notice and unambiguous assent (Berman, Chabolla, Sellers, Kauders), which in practice means a per-user record of what version was shown, when, and how it was accepted, kept for at least Michigan's six-year contract limitations period (MCL 600.5807(9)) after the relationship ends.

### Cited Findings
- E-SIGN validity: "a signature, contract, or other record relating to such transaction may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form" (§ 7001(a)(1)); "a contract relating to such transaction may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because an electronic signature or electronic record was used in its formation" (§ 7001(a)(2)). — [15 U.S.C. § 7001, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/7001) (fetched 2026-09-28).
- E-SIGN consumer consent trigger and elements: § 7001(c)(1) applies where "a statute, regulation, or other rule of law requires that information relating to a transaction or transactions in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce be provided or made available to a consumer in writing"; then the electronic record satisfies the writing requirement only if (A) the consumer has affirmatively consented and not withdrawn consent; (B) before consenting, received a clear and conspicuous statement of the right to have the record on paper, the right to withdraw consent and any conditions/consequences/fees, whether consent applies to one transaction or a category, the procedures to withdraw consent and update contact information, and how to obtain a paper copy and any fee; (C)(i) was provided "a statement of the hardware and software requirements for access to and retention of the electronic records" and (C)(ii) "consents electronically, or confirms his or her consent electronically, in a manner that reasonably demonstrates that the consumer can access information in the electronic form that will be used"; (D) on a material change in hardware/software requirements creating a material risk the consumer cannot access or retain records, the provider gives notice of the revised requirements and the right to withdraw and re-complies with (C). — same LII page (fetched 2026-09-28).
- California ARL retention-capable acknowledgment: a business must "provide an acknowledgment that includes the automatic renewal offer terms or continuous service offer terms, cancellation policy, and information regarding how to cancel in a manner that is capable of being retained by the consumer." — [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17602, Justia 2025](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-7/part-3/chapter-1/article-9/section-17602/); [FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/business-and-professions-code/bpc-sect-17602/) (surfaced by search 2026-09-28; subsection quoted from search extract).
- UETA adoption: UETA has been adopted by 49 states, DC, Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands; New York alone has not, relying on its Electronic Signatures and Records Act (ESRA, 2000), which "does little more than provide that electronic records and signatures are equivalent to paper-based records and signatures." — [NYC Bar, Enacting the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act in New York](https://www.nycbar.org/reports/modernizing-new-york-electronic-signatures-esra-ueta/) and [NYC Bar advocacy page](https://www.nycbar.org/issues-policy/advocacy-campaigns/ueta-esra-new-york-electronic-transactions-law/) (2022–2026); [NYS ITS, ESRA guideline](https://its.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2022/10/nys_g04_001_electronic_signatures_and_records_act_esra.pdf) (Oct. 2022).

Whether the parties agree to conduct a transaction by electronic means is determined from the context and surrounding circumstances, including the parties' conduct." "A party that agrees to conduct a transaction by electronic means may refuse to conduct other transactions by electronic means." — [MCL 450.835](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-450-835) (fetched 2026-09-28).
- Michigan UETA retainability: subsection (3): "If a sender inhibits the ability of a recipient to store or print an electronic record, the electronic record is not enforceable against the recipient." — [MCL 450.838](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-450-838) (fetched 2026-09-28). CONFIRMED present in the Michigan statute, at 450.838(3).
- Michigan UETA retention: "(1) If a law requires that a record be retained, the requirement is satisfied by retaining an electronic record of the information if the record does both of the following: (a) Accurately reflects the information set forth in the record after it was first generated in its final form as an electronic record or otherwise. (b) Remains accessible for later reference." "(2) A requirement to retain a record in accordance with subsection (1) does not apply to any information the sole purpose of which is to enable the record to be sent, communicated, or received." — [MCL 450.842](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-450-842) (fetched 2026-09-28).

### Inferences
- § 7001(c) is not triggered by the license acceptance itself; it is triggered by *legally required* consumer writings. Today the product has none (one-time license, no auto-renewal). Two things would change that: (i) a subscription (state ARLs — California § 17602(a)(3) and its copies — require a retention-capable acknowledgment, which is exactly the kind of "writing" § 7001(c) governs); (ii) any state statute requiring a written refund/return-policy disclosure for the sale channel. If counsel wants belt-and-braces, the § 7001(c)(1) elements can be folded into the first-run flow at low cost: a sentence on the right to a paper copy on request, the right to withdraw consent to electronic records, the scope of consent, the hardware/software needed (a VS Code install and a web browser), and a "reasonable demonstration" step (the user clicks a link that opens the license in the browser or the editor before "Accept" becomes enabled).
- MCL 450.835 agreement to transact electronically is satisfied by conduct here (the whole transaction is in-app), but § 450.838(3) is the live risk: a webview that disables text selection, has no print/save path, and a license URL that later 404s would let a Michigan user argue the record is unenforceable against them. Mechanism: every version of the license lives at a stable, versioned URL (and in the extension bundle as a plain text/Markdown file openable in an editor tab), the dialog has "View license" and "Save a copy," and nothing in the webview CSS blocks selection or copying.
- Retention is an evidentiary problem, not a statutory duty. Every one of Berman/Chabolla/Sellers/Kauders was decided on what the screen looked like and what the click meant; the vendor wins or loses on its ability to reproduce that. Log, per acceptance: user/account id (or license key), UTC timestamp, extension version, license document version and SHA-256 of its text, the 18+ affirmation state, the acceptance method (which button, which UI surface — native notification vs. webview), and the locale. Keep, per license version: the exact dialog source (HTML/strings) and a screenshot of the rendered flow in the default light, dark and high-contrast themes. Retain acceptance logs for the life of the license plus at least six years (MCL 600.5807(9)); retain the per-version artefacts indefinitely. Note that MCL 450.842 also means that if any law later requires retaining the agreement, the electronic log satisfies it so long as it "accurately reflects" the record and "remains accessible."
- Design the acceptance as click-wrap, not sign-in wrap: an explicit, un-prechecked control labelled with the operative words ("I have read and accept the Ashley License Agreement (v3, 2026-09-01)") adjacent to an "Accept" button, with the license reachable in one click. That is the fact pattern Keebaugh upheld and Chabolla/Sellers/Kauders distinguished.

Counsel should confirm a version-proof case if the memo needs one.

## 7.1 Telemetry and error reports: consent standard, California CIPA after SB 690, and FTC order language [PARTIAL — SB 690 status verified; consent case law and FTC orders not]

### Takeaway
No federal or state law verified in this session requires opt-in (as opposed to disclosed, opt-out) consent for diagnostic telemetry collected from adults; the live private-litigation risk has been California's CIPA, used since 2022 against website trackers under Penal Code §631 (wiretap) and, since 2023, §638.51 (pen register / trap and trace). On 28 August 2026 the California Legislature passed SB 690 (Assembly 66–0). As enrolled it amends only Penal Code §637.2 so that a §638.51 claim arising "from conduct occurring on an internet website, online application, or mobile application" may be brought solely by the Attorney General; it applies retroactively to pending claims filed within two years before its 1 January 2027 operative date; §§631 and 632 private claims are untouched, because the 2 July 2026 amendment deleted the earlier "commercial business purpose" exemption. It awaits the Governor (a 30 September 2026 deadline is reported). ## 8.1 PCI DSS scope: which SAQ, and does a hosted checkout or a merchant of record take the vendor out of scope?
### Takeaway
A vendor that never sees a card number is a Level 4 merchant (fewer than 20,000 e-commerce transactions a year) and validates with SAQ A. Since the January 2025 SAQ A revision, the two script-security requirements (6.4.3, 11.6.1) are gone from SAQ A, but a merchant whose page **embeds** the payment fields (an iframe such as Stripe Elements or embedded Checkout) must now affirm that its site "is not susceptible to attacks from scripts" — either by doing 6.4.3/11.6.1-style controls itself or by holding a written confirmation from the payment processor. A **full redirect** to a processor-hosted page (Stripe-hosted Checkout, Payment Links) is outside that new criterion. With a merchant of record, the MoR is the merchant; the vendor has no cardholder-data environment and nothing to attest, though no MoR page fetched this session says that in so many words.

### Cited Findings
- PCI SSC announced on 30 Jan 2025 that the revised SAQ A removes Requirements 6.4.3 (payment-page script management) and 11.6.1 (change/tamper detection on payment pages), plus 12.3.1 (the targeted risk analysis supporting 11.6.1), and adds an eligibility criterion that the merchant "confirm their site is not susceptible to attacks from scripts that could affect the merchant's e-commerce system(s)"; the October 2024 SAQ A is retired 31 Mar 2025 — [PCI SSC blog, "Important Updates Announced for Merchants Validating to SAQ A"](https://blog.pcisecuritystandards.org/important-updates-announced-for-merchants-validating-to-self-assessment-questionnaire-a) (30 Jan 2025); summarised with the same requirement numbers by [SecurityMetrics](https://www.securitymetrics.com/blog/big-changes-for-saq-a) and [HALOCK](https://www.halock.com/pci-ssc-updates-saq-a-removal-of-key-e-commerce-security-requirements-new-eligibility-criteria/) (2025).
- FAQ 1588: the new criterion reads "The merchant has confirmed that their site is not susceptible to attacks from scripts that could affect the merchant's e-commerce system(s)"; a merchant may confirm this by (1) implementing techniques "such as those in PCI DSS Requirements 6.4.3 and 11.6.1" or (2) obtaining confirmation from its payment processor that the "TPSP's/payment processor's solution includes techniques that protect the merchant's payment page from script attacks"; the criterion "applies only to e-commerce merchants using embedded payment pages (iframes). It does NOT apply to merchants using redirects or fully outsourced payment functions"; new SAQ A in force from 1 Apr 2025 — [PCI SSC blog, "FAQ Clarifies New SAQ A Eligibility Criteria for E-Commerce Merchants"](https://blog.pcisecuritystandards.org/faq-clarifies-new-saq-a-eligibility-criteria-for-e-commerce-merchants) (2025).
- Stripe merchant levels: Level 4 = "<20,000 online transactions annually OR up to 1 million total transactions annually"; Level 3 = 20,000–1 million online. "Checkout and Stripe.js and Elements host all card data collection inputs within an iframe served from Stripe's domain (not yours), so your customers' card information never touches your servers" → SAQ A. "Using Stripe.js v2 to pass card data entered in a form hosted on your own site requires completing the SAQ A-EP annually." For Level 3–4 users: "our customized wizard on your PCI Dashboard will ask a series of questions and generate required documentation for you." — [Stripe, "PCI DSS compliance guide"](https://stripe.com/guides/pci-compliance) (accessed 2026-09-28).
- Stripe: "PCI compliance is a shared responsibility... As a business accepting payments, you must do so in a PCI-compliant manner, and annually attest to this compliance." Stripe is a PCI Level 1 Service Provider; payment pages must use TLS 1.2+; card type, last four, expiry are out of scope and may be stored — [Stripe Docs, "Integration security guide"](https://docs.stripe.com/security/guide) (accessed 2026-09-28).
- Paddle: "Paddle is an authorised reseller of Products for Suppliers, which means you purchase the Product from Paddle using our Services"; the Paddle contracting entity is chosen by buyer location (US, Canada, rest of world) — [Paddle Buyer Terms](https://www.paddle.com/legal/buyer-terms) (last updated 31 Mar 2026).
- Paddle: "Paddle.com is the Merchant of Record for all its orders... Paddle is the company you are purchasing from and is responsible for charging you" — [Paddle, "Why has Paddle charged me?"](https://www.paddle.com/about/why-has-paddle-charged-me) (accessed 2026-09-28).
- Lemon Squeezy receipts show "Sold through Link, LLC f/k/a Lemon Squeezy LLC" — [Lemon Squeezy blog, "2026 Update"](https://www.lemonsqueezy.com/blog/2026-update) (28 Jan 2026).

### Inferences
- Stripe **Payment Links** and Stripe-hosted **Checkout** are redirects to checkout.stripe.com (Stripe's own CSP list gives `frame-src https://checkout.stripe.com` for Checkout, i.e. it can also be embedded). Redirect mode = fully outsourced = SAQ A with no FAQ 1588 affirmation. Embedded Checkout or Elements = iframe = SAQ A **plus** the FAQ 1588 affirmation; the easy path is to hold Stripe's written confirmation rather than run script inventories on a one-person site.

## 8.2 Merchant of record mechanics: who is the seller, who owes tax, who handles refunds, and what it costs (Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, FastSpring, Polar vs plain Stripe)
### Takeaway
With Paddle (and the others) the buyer contracts with the MoR, sees the MoR on the card statement, and is refunded under the MoR's buyer terms — Paddle's terms give a 14-day withdrawal right that lapses once the buyer starts downloading or using the product, and otherwise make transactions non-refundable except at Paddle's discretion or where law requires. The MoR calculates, collects and remits sales tax/VAT everywhere; the vendor invoices the MoR. Costs in 2026: Paddle 5% + 50¢ (and products under $10 need custom pricing); Lemon Squeezy 5% + 50¢ but the company is being folded into Stripe Managed Payments (public preview Feb 2026, reported at 3.5% on top of Stripe processing); FastSpring about 5.9% + 95¢ (third-party figure); Polar moved from 4% + 40¢ to 5% + 50¢ for new accounts in May 2026 (third-party figure). ## 8.3 Chargebacks versus the vendor's "no refund for external events" clause
### Takeaway
A card network dispute right belongs to the cardholder and issuer under network rules; the vendor's contract cannot remove it. Under Visa's dispute conditions (13.1 not received, 13.3 not as described/defective, 10.4 card-absent fraud) and Mastercard's 4853 cardholder dispute, the vendor's refund term is only *evidence* in representment, and it counts only if it was disclosed at checkout near the pay button (or on a click-to-accept page) and the vendor can prove the buyer acknowledged it. Stripe's guidance for digital goods is to submit IP, download/activation logs, and a screenshot of how the terms were presented at checkout, and it warns that "even in the most favorable cases, it's very difficult to overturn a disputed payment" (best case about 60%). Stripe charges $15 per dispute received (kept whether you win or not) and a further $15 to counter, returned only on a win. Networks count disputes received, not won: Visa's VAMP excessive threshold for merchants is 1.5% (fraud + disputes ÷ settled CNP transactions) from 1 Apr 2026, with about $8 per item once enrolled. For a low-priced licence, refunding a disgruntled student is almost always cheaper than fighting. US law (FCBA/Reg Z) adds billing-error and claims-and-defenses rights on top, and several states require refund policies to be disclosed before sale, though their reach to online digital licences is unconfirmed.

### Cited Findings
- Visa's guidance for merchants on where to disclose return/refund/cancellation policies: for online orders, on "a sequence of pages before final checkout, with a 'click to accept' or other acknowledgement button", or "on the checkout screen, near the 'submit' or click to accept button"; a link to a separate page is acceptable "if that link forms part of the 'click to accept' acknowledgement"; merchants "have to prove the cardholder received and acknowledged the policy" — [Chargebacks911, "Visa Chargeback Rules"](https://chargebacks911.com/visa-chargeback-rules/) (accessed 2026-09-28), summarising Visa's [Dispute Management Guidelines for Visa Merchants](https://usa.visa.com/dam/VCOM/global/support-legal/documents/merchants-dispute-management-guidelines.pdf) (June 2024 edition; the PDF fetched but its text could not be extracted in this session — see Gaps).
- Stripe: "If your business provides digital goods, include evidence such as an IP address or system log proving the customer downloaded the content or used your software or service"; "provide proof that your customer agreed to and understood your terms of service at checkout ... Include a clean screenshot of how you present your terms of service during checkout, with the relevant policy clearly emphasized"; "Don't ... upload your entire terms of service"; "Disputes incur a dispute fee that still applies if you accept the dispute"; "the card networks don't consider how many disputes you win or lose, only how many you receive"; evidence limits 4.5 MB all networks, 19 pages Mastercard; win-likelihood table 5 dots = 60% down to 1 dot = 5%; Stripe auto-attaches AVS/CVC results and purchase IP; Visa CE 3.0 lets prior non-fraud history with the same cardholder rebut friendly-fraud disputes — [Stripe Docs, "Dispute evidence best practices"](https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/best-practices) (accessed 2026-09-28).

- Paddle (MoR) handles the dispute itself and asks buyers to "contact Paddle directly prior to disputing a Charge" — [Paddle Buyer Terms](https://www.paddle.com/legal/buyer-terms) (31 Mar 2026); Paddle pricing includes "chargeback defense" — [Paddle Pricing](https://www.paddle.com/pricing) (accessed 2026-09-28).
- Federal statute/regulation **[not re-fetched]**: Fair Credit Billing Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1666 (billing-error procedure) — https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1666 ; Regulation Z § 1026.13 (billing errors include goods/services "not accepted by the consumer or the consumer's designee, or not delivered to the consumer as agreed") — https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/13/ ; § 1026.12(c) (cardholder may assert claims and defenses against the issuer where the sale exceeds $50 and occurred in the same state or within 100 miles of the cardholder's address; issuers in practice process network disputes without applying these limits) — https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/12/ .

### Inferences
- The clause "no refund for external events (university changes its systems, VS Code changes its API)" is enforceable as a contract term between vendor and buyer only to the extent state law permits, and is irrelevant to whether an issuer will honour a 13.3 "not as described / defective" dispute. What wins a 13.3 representment is proof the product worked as described *when sold* and that the buyer used it (activation log, feature telemetry if any), plus proof the policy was shown and accepted at checkout. Put the refund policy (in one or two sentences) on the checkout page itself, with a required checkbox, and store the acceptance record (timestamp, IP, policy version) with the order.
- Under Paddle the buyer's contractual refund right is Paddle's: 14 days unless the buyer has started downloading/using it and agreed to immediate access. The vendor's licence should therefore say something like "refunds are handled by our reseller [Paddle] under its refund policy", not state a conflicting "no refunds" rule; the vendor's "external events" language can remain as a description of what is *not* a defect, but cannot override Paddle's discretionary refunds or a statutory right.
- Economically: a $15 non-refundable fee plus a $15 at-risk counter fee on, say, a $29 licence means every dispute costs more than the sale even when won. Set an internal rule to refund on request within some window and reserve disputes for clear fraud.
- VAMP will not touch a vendor doing a few hundred transactions a year in practice — industry summaries describe a minimum monthly count of fraud+dispute items below which Visa does not identify a merchant — but the acquirer (Stripe) can still close an account for a high ratio at any volume.

## 8.4 All-in / "junk fee" pricing laws at checkout
### Takeaway
None of the 2024–2025 all-in pricing laws bites on a single-price, one-time software licence with no mandatory add-on fees. The FTC's Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees (16 CFR part 464) is confined to live-event tickets and short-term lodging (4–1 vote, 17 Dec 2024, effective 12 May 2025); California SB 478 (Civ. Code § 1770(a)(29), 1 Jul 2024) and Minnesota's law (1 Jan 2025) require the advertised price to include all mandatory fees but expressly exclude government taxes, so displaying "$X + applicable tax" and adding Michigan 6% at checkout is compliant. What would break these rules is adding a "processing" or "licensing" fee at the last step that was not in the advertised price.

### Cited Findings
- FTC: the final rule targets "live-event tickets and short-term lodging"; "Industries beyond live-event ticketing and short-term lodging are prohibited from deceiving consumers about fees and pricing per longstanding law"; vote 4–1 with Commissioner Ferguson dissenting; effective 120 days after Federal Register publication (12 May 2025) — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/12/federal-trade-commission-announces-bipartisan-rule-banning-junk-ticket-hotel-fees) (17 Dec 2024).
- California SB 478 / Civ. Code § 1770(a)(29) (effective 1 Jul 2024): unlawful to advertise a price "that does not include all mandatory fees or charges other than ... taxes or fees imposed by a government on the transaction" and reasonable shipping — https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1770 **[not re-fetched]**.
- Minnesota junk-fee law, Minn. Stat. § 325F.69 subd. 8 (effective 1 Jan 2025), similar mandatory-fee inclusion with a tax exclusion — https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325F.69 **[not re-fetched]**.

### Inferences
- MoR checkouts (Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, FastSpring) show the tax-inclusive total once the buyer's location is known; Stripe Checkout with Stripe Tax does the same. Either satisfies the state laws so long as the advertised price on the website is the licence price and the only later addition is tax.
- Avoid pricing the licence net of a "payment processing fee" surcharged at checkout; if a surcharge is ever contemplated, card-network surcharge rules and state anti-surcharge laws come into play — out of scope here.

### Gaps
- New York's 2025–2026 fee-disclosure bills were not researched; nothing found indicates an enacted all-in pricing statute reaching software in New York as of this session.

## 9.1 Advertising the free trial and the "Trial" price label: what must be true and what must be disclosed [PARTIAL — Michigan and California rules verified; federal "free" guides not]

### Takeaway
Two rules were verified. Michigan's MCPA 445.903(1)(r) requires that any representation that goods or services are "free or without charge, or ... words of similar import" be accompanied "clearly and conspicuously ... with equal prominence in immediate conjunction" by "the conditions, terms, or prerequisites to the use or retention" of what is offered — so the trial's length, what happens at expiry and any feature limits must sit next to the word "Trial" / "free" itself, not on a linked page. California's B&P 17500.6 (AB 2426) does not apply to goods offered for "no monetary consideration", so the $0 Trial tier needs no licence acknowledgment, while the paid tier's "Buy" button does (§9.2). A trial that takes no card and never converts is outside ROSCA and every automatic-renewal law (§1.1). The federal 16 CFR 251 "free" guides, the FTC's free-trial guidance, California's §17601 definitions and the New York / Massachusetts advertising rules were not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- MCPA 445.903(1)(r), quoted verbatim in §1.1 and §2.1 — [MCL 445.903](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-445-903) (fetched 2026-09-28).
- AB 2426 / B&P 17500.6 exemptions: the section does not apply to a subscription service that advertises access "solely for the duration of the subscription", to goods offered with "no monetary consideration", or to digital goods available for "permanent offline download to an external storage source to be used without a connection to the internet"; the acknowledgment route requires the purchaser to confirm "that the purchaser is receiving a license to access the digital good", "a complete list of restrictions and conditions of the license", and "that access to the digital good may be unilaterally revoked by the seller if they no longer hold a right to the digital good, if applicable"; the disclosure route requires a statement "before executing each transaction" that the transaction is a licence, with "a hyperlink, QR code, or similar method to access the terms and conditions that provide full details on the license" — [AB-2426 bill text, California Legislative Information](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2426) (fetched 2026-09-28); section text at [B&P 17500.6](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17500.6) (fetched in §9.2); summaries: [Greenberg Traurig (December 2024)](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/12/ab-2426-new-california-law-requires-clear-licensing-disclosures-for-digital-goods), [Thompson Coburn](https://www.thompsoncoburn.com/insights/california-expands-false-advertising-law-to-require-express-licensing-disclosures-in-sales-of-digital-goods/) (search results 2026-09-28; the reported civil penalty of up to $2,500 per violation under the FAL was not verified).
- New York GBL 349(h): the greater of actual damages or $50, trebled up to $1,000 for willful or knowing violations, discretionary attorney's fees; complete defence for conduct that complies with FTC rules (349(d)) — [NY GBL 349](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/349) (fetched 2026-09-28; §2.3). This is the vehicle for a "free"-claim deception suit by a New York student.
- California's retention-capable acknowledgment applies only if an automatic-renewal or continuous-service offer exists — [B&P 17602(a)(3)](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-7/part-3/chapter-1/article-9/section-17602/) (search extract; §6.1).

### Inferences
- A label that satisfies 445.903(1)(r) on its face, in one line adjacent to the tier name on every surface (Marketplace listing, website price table, first-run dialog, checkout): "Trial — free for N days. No card, no automatic charge. On day N+1 Ashley stops working [or: reverts to read-only] until you buy a licence. Trial features: …". The same sentence should appear in all four places; a discrepancy between the listing and the dialog is MCPA 445.903(1)(y) territory (gross discrepancy / failure to provide promised benefits, §2.1).
- Calling the tier "Trial" rather than "Free" does not escape (r): the statute covers "words of similar import", and a $0 tier is free in substance. Conversely, do not describe the paid licence as "free" of anything (e.g. "free updates") without the same adjacent conditions.
- Because AB 2426 exempts $0 goods, the licence-acknowledgment mechanics belong on the paid checkout only; but the trial's first-run dialog is still where the licence is accepted (§6.1), so stating there that the trial is a licence, not a sale, costs nothing and pre-empts the "buy" vocabulary problem when the user upgrades.
- Spread-check the trial funnel after launch the way this project checks any measurement: if a material share of trial users write in asking "why did it stop / was I charged", the adjacent disclosure is failing regardless of what the legal text says.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: 16 CFR 251.1 (especially 251.1(c): all conditions disclosed clearly and conspicuously at the outset, not by footnote; and the rule against "free" where another item's price is raised); FTC business guidance on free trials and negative-option offers; the ".com Disclosures" guide (2013); the 2022 dark-patterns report's treatment of trial disclosures; Cal. B&P 17601 definitions of "free trial" / "free gift"; NY GBL 350 / 350-e; Massachusetts 940 CMR 6.05 on "free" offers; any guidance treating the word "Trial" differently from "Free". No authority was found either way on whether a time-limited full-feature trial versus a permanently feature-limited tier changes the analysis.

## 9.2 Digital-goods "buy vs licence" disclosure laws and marketplace statutes that might name the vendor
### Takeaway
California's AB 2426 (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17500.6, operative 1 Jan 2025) reaches a VS Code extension: a "digital application" is defined as any application a person accesses and manipulates using a computer, and the section makes it unlawful to advertise it with "buy", "purchase" or any term "a reasonable person would understand to confer an unrestricted ownership interest" unless, at each transaction, the seller either obtains an affirmative acknowledgment that the buyer is receiving a licence (with the restrictions listed and, where applicable, that access can be revoked) or gives a clear and conspicuous statement that "buying" is a licence with access to the licence terms. Subscriptions, free goods, and goods provided as a permanent offline download usable without an internet connection are exempt. The INFORM Consumers Act regulates online *marketplaces* with high-volume third-party sellers and does not name a vendor selling its own product through its own checkout. No other enacted state "buy vs licence" statute was found in this session.

### Cited Findings
- BPC § 17500.6: "It shall be unlawful for a seller of a digital good to advertise or offer for sale ... with the terms 'buy,' 'purchase,' or any other term which a reasonable person would understand to confer an unrestricted ownership interest" unless the seller either (A) "receives at the time of each transaction an affirmative acknowledgment from the purchaser" that "(i) the purchaser is receiving a license to access the digital good, (ii) a complete list of restrictions and conditions of the license" (and, if applicable, that access may be unilaterally revoked), or (B) provides before each transaction "a clear and conspicuous statement" that "states in plain language that 'buying' or 'purchasing' the digital good is a license" and provides access to the licence terms; "digital good" means "a digital audiovisual work, digital audio work, digital book, digital code, or digital application or game, whether electronically or digitally delivered or accessed"; "digital application or game" means "any application or game that a person accesses and manipulates using a specialized electronic gaming device, computer, mobile device, tablet, or other device"; exemptions include subscription-based services, free goods, and goods provided as a "permanent offline download" usable without an internet connection; operative 1 Jan 2025 — [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17500.6](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17500.6) (accessed 2026-09-28); [AB 2426 bill text and history](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2426); law-firm summaries: [Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/12/ab-2426-new-california-law-requires-clear-licensing-disclosures-for-digital-goods) (Dec 2024), [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/10/california-becomes-first-state-to-pass-law-targeting-advertising-of-digital-media-licenses) (Oct 2024).
- INFORM Consumers Act, 15 U.S.C. § 45f (effective 27 Jun 2023): obligations fall on "online marketplaces" to collect and verify information from "high-volume third party sellers" (200+ discrete sales and $5,000+ gross revenue in a continuous 12-month period) — https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/integrity-notification-fairness-online-retail-marketplaces-consumers-act **[not re-fetched]**.

### Inferences
- The vendor sells to Michigan students first, but the Marketplace and a hosted checkout are reachable from California; § 17500.6 is triggered by advertising to a California purchaser, so the cheapest course is to comply everywhere. Two compliant patterns: (a) label the button "Get a licence" / "Buy a licence" and place, next to the price and again on the checkout page, a sentence such as "You are buying a licence to use Ashley, not the software itself. The licence is perpetual for the version purchased, non-transferable, and can be terminated for breach of the [Licence Terms] (link)." — this is the § 17500.6(b)(2) "clear and conspicuous statement" route; or (b) add a required checkbox at checkout acknowledging the licence and its restrictions — the (b)(1) affirmative-acknowledgment route. The (b)(2) route is easier on a hosted MoR checkout, which may not allow custom checkboxes.
- The "permanent offline download" exemption may fit if the buyer receives the .vsix and a licence key that keep working with no internet connection, but VS Code extensions are ordinarily updated and installed via the Marketplace, and any online licence check defeats the exemption; do not rely on it without counsel.
- The vendor is not an "online marketplace" under INFORM and Microsoft's Marketplace does not intermediate the payment, so INFORM does not create obligations on the vendor.

### Gaps
- The complete exemption list in § 17500.6 (there may be a further exemption keyed to whether the seller retains the ability to revoke access) was only partially extracted; read the statute text in full before drafting. [Coordinator's verification 2026-09-28: the AB 2426 bill text at leginfo shows three exemptions — subscription access advertised solely for the duration of the subscription, goods offered for no monetary consideration, and permanent offline download to an external storage source usable without an internet connection; no further revocability-keyed exemption appeared in the extracted text. See §9.1.]

## 10.1 OFAC sanctions and EAR export controls for a tiny US software vendor
### Takeaway
A Michigan sole proprietor is a "US person" and is strictly liable for dealings with SDNs and with persons in comprehensively sanctioned jurisdictions (Cuba, Iran, North Korea, and the Crimea, Donetsk and Luhansk regions of Ukraine; Syria's programme was terminated effective 1 Jul 2025 and its regulations removed 26 Aug 2025, though a narrower Assad-focused list-based programme remains and BIS export controls on Syria were relaxed separately). OFAC's 2019 Framework is risk-based: there is no rule that a one-person seller must run a screening system, but every 2020–2026 e-commerce enforcement action (SAP, Tango Card, TradeStation — $1.11 M in March 2026 for about 500 trades from Iran, Syria and Crimea after geo-blocking silently failed) says the same two things: block sanctioned geographies by IP and *test that the block works*. In practice a vendor using Paddle or FastSpring inherits the MoR's country blocking and buyer screening; with Stripe, Stripe screens accounts and refuses restricted-country payments, but the vendor should still geo-block download/activation for the embargoed list and check any manual sales against the free SDN search. Russia is not comprehensively sanctioned; the June 2024 IT-services determination bans IT consultancy/design services and cloud/support services for *enterprise-management* and *design-and-manufacturing* software to persons in Russia, which a student developer tool is not — but the many SDN banks and the EU/UK regimes make Russian sales a counsel question. On the EAR side, a proprietary extension that only calls OS/TLS libraries is most likely EAR99 (or, at most, mass-market 5D992.c with no licence needed to most destinations); it is not "published" because it is sold, so it *is* subject to the EAR, and EAR99 still needs a licence for the embargoed destinations.

### Inferences
- **What OFAC expects of this vendor**: (1) do not sell to the embargoed jurisdictions — enforce by IP geo-block at purchase *and* at download/activation, and re-test the block after every infrastructure change (that is the TradeStation lesson); (2) screen counterparties against the SDN list where names are collected — for a hosted checkout this is done by Stripe or the MoR; for any manual/invoice sale (a department buying seats) run the buyer through Sanctions List Search and keep a screenshot; (3) write a one-page policy stating the above. That is proportionate under the 2019 Framework for a business with a few hundred US student customers.
- With an MoR the vendor is one step removed: Paddle is the seller and blocks sanctioned buyers. The vendor still owns the download/activation endpoints and the Marketplace listing, so it still needs the geo-block there, and Microsoft's publisher terms place export compliance on the publisher.
- Russia: a one-time licence for a student coding assistant is not "IT consultancy", not cloud delivery of ERP/CAD-class software, and not IT support for such software, so the E.O. 14071 determination should not reach it; but payment rails to Russia are largely closed anyway (SDN banks; Visa/Mastercard suspended Russia in 2022), and if any part of Ashley runs as a hosted service the "cloud-based services" prong needs a closer look. Counsel decision; the simplest policy is to geo-block Russia and Belarus too.
- EAR: because the extension is sold under licence it is not "published" and therefore is subject to the EAR; if it implements no cryptography of its own and only uses OS/VS Code-provided TLS, the ancillary-cryptography/primary-function analysis points to EAR99, in which case no classification request or self-classification report is needed and no licence is needed except to embargoed destinations (and subject to Russia/Belarus-specific software restrictions). If it bundles its own crypto library it is likely 5D992.c mass-market, still shippable to most destinations under 740.17(b)(1) but with a self-classification report obligation. Counsel/consultant should sign off on the classification once the final dependency list is known.

## Summary of what this file covers and what it does not

This file assembles, as of 2026-09-28, the regulators and private rights of action that can reach a Michigan sole-proprietor vendor selling per-person licences for a VS Code extension to students anywhere in the US, and the disclosure, consent or mechanism that answers each. It is complete and primary-sourced for: the seven state consumer-protection statutes most likely to be invoked (Michigan MCPA, California CLRA/UCL/FAL and Civ. Code 1670.8, New York GBL 349, Illinois ICFA, New Jersey CFA/TCCWNA, Massachusetts 93A, Texas DTPA), their pre-suit notice and tender mechanics, the clause traps (non-disparagement under CRFA and Civ. Code 1670.8, liquidated damages, "void where prohibited" under TCCWNA, public-injunction waivers), Michigan's Restatement §187 choice-of-law rule and post-Briskin personal jurisdiction, AAA consumer-arbitration fees and click-wrap assent cases through Chabolla (2025); COPPA (including the April 2025 amendments), FERPA and the K-12-only state student-privacy statutes, the infancy doctrine with Michigan's MCL 600.1403 and 722.52, ADA Title III's circuit split with DOJ's 2022 guidance still live and the Title II / HHS 504 deadlines as extended in April–May 2026, Unruh and New York exposure, and a WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA floor for the first-run dialog; E-SIGN §7001(a)/(c) and Michigan UETA including the MCL 450.838(3) store-or-print rule and a retention design keyed to MCL 600.5807(9); PCI SAQ A after the January 2025 revision, merchant-of-record mechanics and fees, Michigan's taxation of downloaded prewritten software, chargeback rights that no contract term removes, Visa VAMP at 1.5% from April 2026, the junk-fee laws' inapplicability, OFAC's risk-based expectations illustrated by the March 2026 TradeStation settlement, the EAR position, and California's AB 2426 "buy = licence" disclosure.
